# Garden Infiltration level design

`pp_garden_infiltration` is the first campaign mission, Operation Garden Ghost. A tiny squad crosses an oversized backyard garden, learns to fight with the pistol and shotgun, and breaches the sandbox wall. The mission starts with room to look around. It ends by asking players to choose how to cross a defended space with the tools they just learned.

Use this document to guide future Plastic Platoon levels and to evaluate edits to Garden. The route below is a design reading of the current map and the designer's playthrough intent. Treat its encounter order as the expected first run. Enemy activation, exact lines of sight, and route choices can vary in play.

## Scale, visibility, and pacing

Build the space from things a tiny soldier would recognize at a gigantic scale. In Garden, raised beds set the overall route, dirt banks turn it, grass establishes scale at eye level, and a half-buried bucket makes a memorable pickup location. In a toy-heavy setting, a single oversized toy can do the same work as a garden landmark. Give prominent props a job in navigation, cover, interaction, or discovery where the setting supports it.

Garden's winding corridors serve three purposes. The long runs feel like the spaces between raised beds. The bends and solid earth banks limit how much of the map a player can see at once and help contain the potentially visible set (PVS) produced by VIS. The same bends separate combat from quieter pockets where the player can recover, search, and read the next route. Check those three effects together when opening a sightline or adding a shortcut. Decorative foliage can guide the eye, while structural geometry should carry the visibility work. ## Final fight and cover layout

The earlier fights teach players to advance, fire, and recover. The last yard asks them to decide how much ground to give up to the machine gunners. The two baseline positions are `(4096, 4544, 216)` and `(4272, 5224, 216)`. Their intended role is intermittent suppressive fire across the yard. Shotgun soldiers around ground-level cover punish a blind rush or a player staying focused on the high positions. Expect different kill orders and changes of plan as enemies move. [Finale enemy and barrel placements](source/pp_garden_infiltration.map#L5924)

| Intended approach | What the layout needs to support |
| --- | --- |
| **Close with the shotgun** | A chain of reachable cover with readable gaps in enemy fire. The route should get the player into effective shotgun range while exposing them to close ambushes and barrel hazards along the way. |
| **Pick off the gunners with pistol ADS** | Protected peeking positions with a clear shot to the high threats and a way to break sight again. This is intended to be the safer solution once spotted. Let players discover it by reading the yard and trying the pistol's aim-down-sights mode. |

Borrow from paintball field design. Start from the entrance angle you can predict, then provide several navigable lanes and short moves between cover. Each piece has a coverage direction. Full cover breaks the relevant sightline, partial cover lets a player fire over it and duck, and intermediate cover can protect against one angle while exposing another. The designer's rough estimate of 80 grid units for partial cover is a blockout reference. On level ground the default player eye heights are 46 units standing and 22 crouched. Set cover from the local floor and check both eye and muzzle clearance against the actual firing angles. [Player hull and view heights](../src/common/pmove.c#L1033)

Static traces on the published BSP illustrate the directional cover. At `y = 4800`, a standing eye at `x = 3360` has a clear ray to both baseline gunners. At `x = 3700`, terrain screens both. At `x = 4160`, the ray clears to the northern gunner only. These samples support the intended changes in exposure across the yard. Playtesting is needed to compare the safety of the two approaches once the enemies start moving and firing. [Published BSP record](garden-infiltration-status.json)

Give players and enemies usable width on the main and side paths. Provide room to turn, pass, and back out when a lane becomes dangerous. Place a few defenders where the surrounding cover makes their position look deliberate. Each enemy role should have a spatial reason, whether that is an ambush around a turn, a patrol crossing a lane, an assault down a run, or a high sentry denying an exposed crossing. Garden's main route is linear and its fights still give players choices about how to move through them.

## Rewards and campaign progression

- **Give basic recovery a predictable place.** Add health, armor, and relevant ammo in quiet pockets after a stressful beat. Keep the opening sparse, then increase supply density as players gain weapons and take more damage. Check the team's effective supply in co-op as well as a solo player's inventory.
- **Make searching pay.** Inspect corners, behind large props, and reachable ledges from the player's view. Put desirable extras where finding or reaching them feels like an accomplishment. A peek behind a prop, a climb, a committed jump, or a small interaction can earn a stronger reward. Use a short trail of small health when you want to draw the player's eye or feet toward a route. Give an expected weapon unlock a stronger cue, as Garden does with the shape and lighting of the bucket.
- **Plan the arsenal across a campaign series.** Use the Quake and Quake II progression the designer described as a pacing reference. Introduce roughly one or two standard guns per level, with room to practice each. Aim for the full arsenal around level seven or eight, then give players fights in the closing levels, such as eight through ten, that use its breadth. Treat those numbers as a campaign planning target.
- **Let secrets bend the schedule.** An early powerful weapon can offer a strong optional advantage and new tactical choices. Keep its compatible ammo scarce at that point and balance the critical path around the expected loadout. A player who finds it should have a reason to choose when to spend it. Duke Nukem and Doom are the designer's references for this style of reward.
- **Escalate through composition.** Use groups of individually weak enemies for the sense of being overwhelmed and then regaining control. Reserve tougher enemies and concentrated pressure for climaxes and bosses where they change how the player fights. Follow dense fights with room to breathe, restock, and explore. Test changes to enemy counts alongside cover, enemy access, and the ammunition available at that point.

## Teaching through the environment

Teach an interaction where the player can see the obstacle and the result together. Repeat it soon enough that the earlier lesson helps solve a new problem. Garden uses a nearby ant to give immediate meaning to a barrel blast, then puts the same visual cue against a visibly cracked wall. Give required interactions an obvious first example and a legible result. Favor route shape, recognizable props, light, sound, and the state change itself. In-world labels should make sense as part of the setting, such as the label on a soap bottle.

Future levels can apply the same sequence to a shootable faucet that fills a sink and lifts the player to an exit, an RC car that carries a rider when its controls are hit, or a garage remote that opens the door. Introduce the object and its obvious effect before asking players to combine it with other mechanics. Supported interactions can also hide secrets and create tactical opportunities. An early bunker could teach indirect damage with barrels and a breakable wall. A later bunker could make its firing aperture hard to attack directly and reward lobbing grenades or using a flamethrower, once those options are available. These are candidate designs.

## Subsequent missions

Let Garden's breach carry the squad into Sandbox Assault as the next part of the same operation. Use a dropship when insertion contributes to the mission setup. An entry from the previous breach can give the next map an immediate spatial and narrative connection.

Treat the work-in-progress `pp_sandbox_assault` as a contrast in layout. Use connected rooms, elevation changes, and combat from several angles. Sandbox's editable source already places enemies at several heights. Build on that with branches that rejoin at meaningful positions and give players control over which entrances and elevations they use. Give a room entrance enough cover, visibility, or a fallback so the player can assess a threat above or to the side before committing. Budget simultaneous views and enemy activation when several rooms connect. [Sandbox Assault work-in-progress source](source/pp_sandbox_assault.map#L3285)

## Blockout and playtest review

1. **Lay out the beats.** Sketch arrival, encounters, breaks, discoveries, and the exit. Assign a purpose and expected pressure to each fight. Record what the player probably holds, how they got it, and what the fight asks them to do differently. Include the final combat problem and the ending before investing in detail.
2. **Test the space with rough geometry.** Walk the main route and side lanes from the player camera. Test enemy and player traversal, useful cover on each approach, sightlines from high threats, and retreats. Check VIS and PVS on representative views after structural changes. For target-device performance, use a current build and check split-screen views both in one fight and across different regions.
3. **Place landmarks, supplies, and interactions.** Use the large forms to orient players and make routes readable. Place the baseline resources, then add deliberate finds behind cover, around corners, and above the main path. Play early weapon pickups forward into their first good use, and make sure difficulty and co-op leave enough resources to learn.
4. **Run the mission blind and test its branches.** For Garden, observe whether a new player can orient at spawn, hit the ants, find the shotgun, recover after combat, learn what barrels do, and recognize the breach. Try the shotgun approach and the pistol ADS approach separately on the finale. Check the effect of extra Hard enemies, shared supply and weapon access in co-op, and whether a player can enter the transition volume before opening the wall. Verify the destination starts correctly from the actual packaged content.
